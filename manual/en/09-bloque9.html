9.1What this block fits

A distribution model learns to tell the environment of the sites where the species was recorded from the environment available in the region, and then applies what it has learnt to every cell of the map. The result is a continuous surface of suitability: how similar the climate of each cell is to that of the occupied sites.

The key word is available. Since there are no true absences (section 1.1), the contrast is made against a sample of the environment of the whole study area, the background points. That is why the area you choose determines what the species is compared against, and why Block 2 mattered so much.

9.2Preparing the data

The Prepare model data → button does four things and reports on them:

  1. It crops to the chosen extent. By default this is the country of the records —or the states from Block 2, if you chose them deliberately—. Records outside it do not go in, and the app says how many there are.
  2. It reads the layers onto a grid. The grid inherits the resolution of your layers; if the number of cells is huge, the app aggregates blocks of cells so that the computation is feasible in the browser.
  3. It thins the presences. With the Keep a single presence per grid cell checkbox —ticked by default— fifty records of the same tree count once. It is the most effective defence against sampling bias.
  4. It draws the background points spread across the study area, with a fixed seed so that the result is reproducible.
▸The example, prepared

With the Mexican pinyon and the WorldClim layers at 10 arc-minutes: extent Country of the records (MX), box from −118.6° to −86.4° and from 14.4° to 32.8°, a grid of 194 × 111 cells of 18.6 km, 8 predictors. Of the 1,885 cleaned records, 14 fall outside the extent —those from the United States above the 32.8° parallel— and of the remaining 1,871, 1,468 fall in a cell that is already occupied or has no data: that leaves 403 presences against 7,751 background points.

Seeing 1,871 records turn into 403 presences is alarming the first time. No data have been lost: the repetition has been removed. With 18.6 km cells, 403 is the number of distinct cells where the species has been recorded, and that is what the model can learn from.

9.3The ten algorithms

AlgorithmWhat it does and when it is a good choice
MaxEnt-stylePenalised (L1) logistic regression on linear, quadratic, product and hinge terms, with cloglog output. It is equivalent to maximum entropy modelling and is the sensible starting point in almost any study.
GLMGeneralised linear model with linear and quadratic terms. Transparent, interpretable, and the most sensitive to collinearity.
GAMGeneralised additive model with splines: smooth curves without imposing their shape.
Random forestMany trees with balanced subsampling. Flexible and robust; its validation uses out-of-bag predictions so as not to inflate the fit.
Boosted trees (BRT)Trees added one at a time, each correcting the error of the previous ones. Very accurate, and the most prone to overfitting.
Support vector machineNon-linear boundary with a radial kernel.
Neural networkFive small networks, averaged.
BioclimThe classic climatic envelope: percentiles of each variable. Simple and useful with very few presences.
DomainGower distance to the most similar record.
MahalanobisDistance to the environmental centroid, taking the covariance into account.

9.4Validation

θWhy spatial blocks and not random splits

If the records are split at random, the test points end up a few kilometres from the training points: the model gets them right because they are on the same hill, not because it has learnt anything general. The result is a high and misleading AUC. Spatial block validation divides the area into blocks and leaves out whole blocks, so the model has to predict in areas it has not seen. The values drop, and that is as it should be.

MetricWhat it measures and how to read it
AUCThe probability that a presence site receives a higher suitability than a background site drawn at random. 0.5 is chance. Usual reading bands: 0.7–0.8 acceptable, 0.8–0.9 good, above 0.9 excellent. They are conventions, and with presence-only data the AUC depends on the study area: values are not compared across different studies.
TSSSensitivity + specificity − 1, at the threshold that maximises it. From −1 to 1; by convention, above 0.6 is considered good. It is more stable than kappa and does not depend on prevalence.
Boyce indexMeasures whether the higher suitability classes contain proportionally more presences: it is the metric designed for presence-only data. It ranges from −1 to 1; above 0.8 indicates a very consistent model.
Omission (P10)The fraction of test presences that falls below the 10th-percentile threshold. Ideally close to 0.1.

9.5Reading the metrics

▸What these numbers say
  • The four models are practically equal in test AUC: MaxEnt-style 0.840, GLM 0.840, random forest 0.837 and BRT 0.833, with standard deviations between ±0.016 and ±0.022. When four different algorithms agree like this, the signal is in the data, not in the method.
  • BRT overfits the most: its training AUC is 0.928 against 0.833 in testing, a gap of almost 0.10. The random forest (0.874 against 0.837) and the GLM, the most rigid (0.885 against 0.840), have the smallest gaps.
  • The Boyce index separates what the AUC does not: the random forest reaches 0.958 and BRT stays at 0.856. With almost identical AUCs, the forest ranks the suitability classes better.
  • The TSS lies between 0.51 and 0.54, below the 0.6 usually asked for: a reminder that with 403 presences and 18.6 km cells the model is useful, not perfect.

9.6Importance and response curves

Two different questions: how much each variable contributes, and how the species responds to it.

!Importance is not causation

That elevation comes out as the most important variable in all four models does not mean that elevation itself causes the distribution: it may be a variable that travels with the true cause —the cold, the moisture of the mountains, the soil, history—. And if two variables are correlated, the model shares the importance between them arbitrarily; that is why Block 6 comes before this one.

9.7The maps and the threshold

The app draws three maps per model: the continuous suitability, the presence/absence map after applying a threshold, and the uncertainty between models when there are several.

ThresholdWhat it does
Max. TSS (sens + spec)The one that maximises sensitivity + specificity. It is the most commonly used and the one the app proposes.
10th percentile of presencesThe P10 threshold leaves out the 10% of presences with the lowest suitability. Useful when you suspect some records are poorly georeferenced.
Minimum training presenceKeeps every presence. The most permissive, and the most sensitive to a single error.
Sensitivity = specificityBalances the two kinds of error.

With the threshold applied, the app computes the suitable area in square kilometres: in the example, the MaxEnt-style model with the maximum-TSS threshold (0.384) marks 471,566 km² of Mexican territory with a climate suitable for the pinyon. That figure changes with the threshold, so you must report which one you used.

9.8The ensemble

When several algorithms agree, their average usually predicts better than any of them on its own, and their disagreement is an honest measure of uncertainty. The ensemble combines the models by weighted mean, median or committee average (votes), and weights them by test AUC, TSS, Boyce or equally. Next to the averaged map, the app draws the agreement map: where the models say the same thing and where they do not.

9.9Projection to the future

The last section applies the fitted model to future climate layers. Scenarios are added to a list —each with its climate model, its pathway and its period— and they are all projected in one go.

What it deliversWhat it is for
Map per scenarioThe future suitability, with the same threshold as the present.
Gain and lossHow much area is gained, how much is lost and how much is kept, in km² and as a percentage.
Shift of the suitable areaHow far the centre of the distribution moves, in which direction, and how much it climbs in elevation.
Dispersal assumptionsUnlimited, none, or limited to a distance: three different answers to “will it be able to get there?”.
RefugiaThe areas that are suitable today and in every future scenario: the first thing to conserve.
Ensemble across scenariosMean and agreement between climate models, which is where the greatest uncertainty lies.
!Never publish a projection without its MESS

The MESS map marks the cells where the future climate falls outside the range the model saw during training. There the prediction is not an estimate: it is the formula extended blindly. The app hatches those areas on the future maps and lets you export the MESS separately. Without that layer, a suitability map for 2070 promises a precision it does not have.

9.10Common problems

What you seeWhat happened and what to do
The map does not cover the whole countryThe grid was prepared with a different extent. Change the extent and press Prepare model data → again: the app warns you when the prepared grid no longer matches the chosen extent.
An AUC of 0.99Be suspicious before celebrating: it usually comes from random validation instead of blocks, from a huge study area, or from duplicated presences.
Very few presences after preparing, or a model that takes too longThe first is the per-cell thinning: if it leaves you below fifteen presences, use higher-resolution layers or enlarge the area. The second is normal for the random forest and the boosted trees, the most expensive: start with MaxEnt and GLM.
There are suitable areas with no recordsIt may be an interesting prediction —unexplored habitat— or extrapolation: check it against the MESS and against what you know about the species.
The future map is almost all suitableCheck that the future layers have the same resolution and the same variables as those of the present, and look at the MESS.
