3.1Why clean

Biodiversity databases accumulate two centuries of work done with very different means: handwritten labels, localities written from memory, georeferencing done afterwards from a text, hurried digitisation. The resulting errors are not random: they recur in the same forms, and that is why they can be searched for with rules.

What is at stake is not how the map looks. A record bearing the coordinates of the centre of the country brings into the model a climate the species never occupied; fifty duplicate records of the same tree tell the algorithm that this climate is fifty times more important than the rest; a specimen grown in a European garden widens the niche until it becomes unrecognisable.

θDuplicates carry weight

A presence-only model learns from the contrast between where there are records and what the environment is like in general. If a site appears fifty times —because fifty people photographed the same tree next to the viewpoint— the model takes that environment to be fifty times more common than it is. Removing duplicates is not cosmetic tidying: it stops sampling effort from passing itself off as ecological preference.

3.2The nine rules

Each rule is switched on or off separately and shows how many records it would flag before you apply anything. That live count is the useful part: seeing that a rule flags 400 records forces you to understand why before you delete them.

RuleWhat it looks for and when to switch it off
Missing or out-of-range coordinatesLatitude or longitude empty, containing text, or impossible (|lat| > 90, |lon| > 180). There is no reason to switch it off.
Coordinate (0, 0)The “null island” in the Gulf of Guinea, which appears when a program writes zeros instead of leaving the field empty.
Latitude ≈ longitudeBoth values almost identical and far from zero: the pattern left by copying one cell over the other. Since it can genuinely occur along a diagonal band of the planet, the app treats it as informative.
GBIF geospatial issuesGBIF’s own flags: country not matching the coordinate, presumably swapped coordinate, and others. Informative, because some flags are harmless.
Exact coordinate duplicatesRecords with the same latitude and longitude, down to the last decimal. The first is kept. It is usually the rule that removes the most records.
Duplicates after roundingRecords that coincide after rounding to N decimals: the same tree recorded with different precisions. With 4 decimals, “the same site” means about 11 m.
Low precisionDeclared uncertainty above the threshold, or coordinates with two decimals or fewer —which already amounts to more than a kilometre. Informative, because its threshold depends on the resolution of your layers.
Country centroidPoints within about 10 km of the geographic centre of the stated country: the classic mark of a record georeferenced “at country level”.
Spatial outliersRecords whose distance to the centre of the point cloud exceeds k times the interquartile range, computed per taxon. It detects whatever lies far from where everything else is.

3.3The three parameters

ParameterWhat it changes
Decimals to detect duplicates after rounding
default 4		Defines how close counts as “the same site”: 4 decimals ≈ 11 m, 3 ≈ 110 m, 2 ≈ 1.1 km. With 1 km layers it makes sense to go down to 2; with 18 km layers, like those of the example, the thinning by cell in Block 9 does that job better.
Uncertainty threshold
default 10,000 m		The declared uncertainty above which a record is considered low precision. A sensible rule: the threshold should not be larger than the cell size of your layers.
Factor k for outliers
default 3		How many interquartile ranges of distance are tolerated. Lowering it to 1.5 flags many more records —and starts flagging real populations—; raising it to 5 lets almost everything through.

3.4Informative rules: flagging without deleting

Three rules come switched off on purpose. It is not that they matter less: it is that the decision depends on your study. A record with 5 km of uncertainty is useless for a 1 km model and perfectly usable for an 18 km one. The app tells you how many there are and you decide.

!Never delete without looking

A spatial outlier may be an error… or the most interesting disjunct population in your study. Before applying the rule, go to the Block 4 map and look at where the flagged points are. If they are real populations, switch the rule off and remove by hand the records that really are errors. The manual insists: the rules propose, the researcher disposes.

3.5The before-and-after report

When you press Apply cleaning → the report appears: how many records entered, how many remained, how many left and through which rule.

▸The example, rule by rule

With the default rules, the 2,400 records of the Mexican pinyon come down to 1,885 (78.5% kept). The 515 that go are: 407 exact coordinate duplicates, 97 duplicates after rounding to four decimals and 11 spatial outliers. The other rules flagged nothing: there are no impossible coordinates, no (0, 0) and no country centroids.

The informative rules also pointed out 103 low-precision records, which were kept. With 18 km cells they do no harm; with 1 km layers it would be worth reviewing them.

▸The eleven outliers, one by one

It is worth seeing who the 11 records removed by the outlier rule are, because they explain what it is for:

  • Nine are trees cultivated outside the Americas: six in New Zealand, two in France and one in Austria. The rule found them without knowing anything about countries or cultivation: they are simply very far from the rest of the cloud.
  • One is at (23.000, −102.000), whole degrees, in the middle of Zacatecas. It is a record of Pinus cembroides subsp. orizabensis, whose other 65 records are in Puebla (38), Veracruz (19), Tlaxcala (5), San Luis Potosí (3) and Querétaro (1). A coordinate in round degrees, hundreds of kilometres from its population, is a placeholder that someone never corrected.
  • The last one is another orizabensis, on the boundary between Guanajuato and Querétaro, also outside the range of the subtaxon.

The fact that the rule works per taxon is what makes the second finding possible: (23, −102) lies within the range of the species, but very far from that of its subspecies.

3.6What cleaning does not do

  • It does not check identifications. If someone confused two pines, the record has a perfect coordinate and a wrong name, and no geographic rule will detect it. That can only be fixed by examining specimens or by trusting sources.
  • It does not correct sampling bias. Removing exact duplicates helps, but the build-up of records along roads is still there. Thinning to one presence per cell is done in Block 9, and is explained there.
  • It does not judge age. If you are interested in the recent climate, filter by year in Block 2.
  • It does not decide for you. Everything it does is recorded in the report table, and the raw set remains available for download.

3.7What to download

FileWhat it is for
⬇ Raw CSVEverything downloaded, neither filtered nor cleaned. It is your backup: keep it together with the date of the query.
⬇ Clean CSVThe set the following blocks will work with. It is what goes into the supplementary material of a paper.
⬇ Clean GeoJSONThe same points as a geographic layer, to open them in a mapping program.

3.8Common problems

What you seeWhat happened and what to do
More than half of the records goThey are almost always exact duplicates, and that is almost always fine. Check the report table: if most of them left as duplicates, your set had many observations of the same site.
The outlier rule flags populations you know to be realRaise the factor k, or switch the rule off and remove by hand whatever really is an error. With species of disjunct distribution —those of isolated mountain ranges— it is always worth reviewing it.
All the counts are zeroYour data were already clean, or you are cleaning a small set of your own. It is not an error.
Fewer records remain than you needGo back to Block 2 and enlarge the area or the period before lowering the demands of the cleaning.
I want to know which ones leftDownload the raw and the clean CSV and compare them by record identifier: the difference is the discarded records.
iFor the methods section

One sentence is enough, and with these data it writes itself: “Of the 2,400 records downloaded from GBIF, 407 exact coordinate duplicates, 97 duplicates at four decimals and 11 spatial outliers (k = 3, per taxon) were removed, giving a final set of 1,885 records”.
